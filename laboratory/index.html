NHANES Laboratory Data

From specimen collection to analysis-ready evidence

Explore how NHANES laboratory data are collected, organized, documented, validated, and transformed into defensible analytical datasets.
Laboratory science · survey data · reproducibility

Laboratory results become evidence only after their context is preserved.

NHANES laboratory files connect specimen collection, analytical methods, quality assurance, participant eligibility, survey design, and public-use data. This section explains how to move from a released laboratory file to a scientifically defensible analytical dataset.

Specimen context Collection, handling, storage, and eligibility
Method context Instrument, procedure, calibration, and quality control
Data context Variables, units, detection limits, and missingness
Survey context Weights, strata, PSUs, cycles, and target population

The laboratory data journey

Laboratory data pass through several scientific and operational stages before appearing in an NHANES XPT file.

1
Eligibility Determine who qualifies for the examination or laboratory component.
2
Collection Obtain blood, urine, or another specimen under defined procedures.
3
Handling Process, label, store, and transport the specimen appropriately.
4
Measurement Analyze the specimen using a documented laboratory method.
5
Quality review Apply calibration, controls, flags, and analytical validation.
6
Public release Publish variables, documentation, weights, and analytical guidance.
ImportantThe released value is the final stage, not the whole story

A laboratory result should be interpreted together with:

  • who was eligible;
  • how the specimen was collected;
  • how the method worked;
  • what units were reported;
  • whether limits of detection applied;
  • which survey weight represents the tested population.

Four laboratory-data principles

Methods matter

The same analyte may be measured using different instruments, procedures, calibration systems, or laboratory sites across survey cycles.

Eligibility defines the sample

Not every participant is eligible for every laboratory component. Age, examination status, fasting, session, or subsample selection may apply.

Units must be verified

A familiar variable name does not guarantee that units, coding, reporting limits, or interpretation remained unchanged.

Provenance must survive

Every analytical record should retain its survey cycle, source file, component, variable origin, and processing history.

Explore laboratory component types

Select a category to see how its analytical considerations may differ.

Hematology

Hematology components may include complete blood count variables such as hemoglobin, hematocrit, erythrocyte indices, leukocyte counts, and platelets.

  • Review analyzer and method changes across cycles.
  • Verify units and age-specific interpretation.
  • Distinguish primary and repeat examinations.
  • Retain specimen and cycle provenance.

Clinical chemistry

Chemistry components may include glucose, lipids, renal markers, liver-associated measurements, electrolytes, and other biomarkers.

  • Determine whether fasting or examination-session restrictions apply.
  • Review specimen type and analytical platform.
  • Identify component-specific subsample weights.
  • Check method-comparison or calibration guidance.

Environmental exposure biomarkers

Exposure components may measure metals, pesticides, volatile compounds, metabolites, or other environmental biomarkers.

  • Review limits of detection and substitution rules.
  • Identify dilution adjustments where relevant.
  • Check batch, specimen, and storage considerations.
  • Confirm the applicable laboratory subsample weight.

Infectious disease testing

Infectious disease components may include antibody, antigen, molecular, or other evidence of infection or immunity.

  • Understand qualitative and quantitative result coding.
  • Review confirmatory testing algorithms.
  • Distinguish current infection, past exposure, and immunity.
  • Check age and specimen-availability restrictions.

Nutrition biomarkers

Nutrition laboratory components may include vitamins, minerals, metabolites, and other indicators of nutritional status.

  • Review fasting, supplement use, and collection timing.
  • Check specimen stability and assay changes.
  • Assess biomarker-specific reference considerations.
  • Identify the appropriate survey weight and analytical sample.

Laboratory detection limits

Some laboratory components include values below a method’s detection limit.

The released data may provide:

  • a reported result variable;
  • a detection-limit indicator;
  • a lower detection limit;
  • an imputed or substituted result;
  • cycle-specific analytical guidance.
WarningDo not invent a universal substitution rule

Rules such as:

LOD / √2

or:

LOD / 2

may be appropriate in some contexts but should not be applied automatically.

Use the component documentation, analytical objective, distribution, and sensitivity analysis to justify the treatment of values below detection.

Missing laboratory values

A missing result may indicate:

  • the participant was not eligible;
  • the participant did not attend the MEC examination;
  • the specimen was not collected;
  • specimen volume was insufficient;
  • the assay failed;
  • the result was excluded during quality review;
  • the analyte was not measured in that cycle;
  • the participant was not selected for the subsample.

The meaning of missingness should be investigated before excluding records.

Common laboratory-data mistakes

WarningMistake 1: Selecting a variable by name alone

The variable’s method, unit, eligibility, and cycle documentation must also be reviewed.

WarningMistake 2: Assuming every participant has every laboratory result

Laboratory eligibility, examination completion, specimen availability, and subsampling affect record availability.

WarningMistake 3: Combining cycles without reviewing method changes

A consistent variable name does not prove analytical comparability.

WarningMistake 4: Ignoring detection-limit variables

Reported values near or below detection may require specific analytical treatment.

WarningMistake 5: Using the wrong survey weight

The weight must represent the smallest eligible sample associated with the laboratory variables used.

Laboratory-data knowledge check

NoteIs a laboratory variable name sufficient to establish comparability across cycles?

No. Review the method, units, eligibility, instrument, documentation, and cycle-specific analytical guidance.

NoteWhy might a participant have no laboratory result?

They may have been ineligible, absent from the examination, missing a specimen, excluded during quality review, or outside the selected subsample.

NoteShould repeat examinations be appended automatically to the primary laboratory file?

No. Their analytical role and eligibility must be investigated first.

NoteWhat information should remain attached to an imported laboratory record?

At minimum, retain the survey cycle, source file, component, participant identifier, and file role.

NoteDoes a correct assay result automatically produce a valid population estimate?

No. Population inference also requires correct eligibility, data integration, survey weighting, and design-based analysis.
