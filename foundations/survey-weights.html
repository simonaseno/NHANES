NHANES Survey Weights

Select, interpret, and apply weights that match the analytical population

Learn why NHANES survey weights are needed, how interview, examination, and subsample weights differ, and how to select an appropriate weight for an analysis.

A survey weight connects one participant to the population

An NHANES participant does not necessarily represent only one person.

A survey weight reflects the participant’s contribution to a population estimate after accounting for factors such as:

  • probability of selection;
  • oversampling;
  • survey nonresponse;
  • adjustment to population controls.

The correct weight is determined by the variables, component, eligible sample, and survey cycles used in the analysis.

Four ideas to remember

1

Weights represent people

A participant with a larger weight represents more people in the target population.

2

Weights are not interchangeable

Interview, examination, fasting, laboratory, and other subsample weights represent different eligible populations.

3

The narrowest sample governs

The analysis usually uses the weight associated with the smallest eligible subsample represented by all included variables.

4

Cycles affect the weight

When multiple cycles are combined, the weight generally requires an appropriate multi-cycle adjustment.

From research question to weight

Question Define the target population and estimand.
Variables List every variable required in the analysis.
Eligibility Identify the smallest eligible subsample.
Weight Select and document the corresponding weight.

What a survey weight does

A participant’s final weight may reflect several adjustments.

  • Selection probability
  • Oversampling
  • Nonresponse
  • Population controls

People do not all have the same probability of entering the sample.

The inverse of the selection probability is an important starting point for a survey weight.

A person selected with a lower probability may receive a larger base weight.

Some population groups are deliberately sampled at higher rates.

Weights correct the final contribution of those participants so oversampled groups do not become overrepresented in population estimates.

Not everyone selected for NHANES completes every interview, examination, or laboratory component.

Weights may include adjustments intended to reduce bias associated with nonresponse.

Weights may be calibrated to population totals so the weighted sample aligns with known demographic distributions.

Core principle: Survey weights are part of the study design. They are not arbitrary multipliers added after the analysis.

Common categories of NHANES weights

Interview weight

Used when the analysis is based only on variables available for the interview sample.

WTINT2YR

MEC examination weight

Used for variables measured among participants examined in the mobile examination center.

WTMEC2YR

Subsample weight

Used when a laboratory test or procedure was performed only in a smaller eligible subsample.

Component-specific

Interview weights

Interview weights are generally associated with participants who completed the household interview.

They may be appropriate when every variable in the analysis comes from interview data.

Examples may include analyses based on:

  • age;
  • sex;
  • questionnaire responses;
  • interview-based health information.

An interview weight is not automatically appropriate when the analysis also includes examination or laboratory variables.

Examination weights

The mobile examination center, or MEC, sample is smaller than the interviewed sample because not every interviewed participant completes the examination.

The MEC weight is commonly used when the analysis includes:

  • examination measures;
  • body measurements;
  • blood pressure;
  • laboratory measurements collected through the MEC examination process.

A CBC analysis often begins by evaluating whether the MEC examination weight is appropriate.

The documentation for the specific component and cycle must still be reviewed.

Subsample weights

Some tests are conducted only among a subset of examined participants.

Examples may involve:

  • fasting laboratory tests;
  • morning-session examinations;
  • selected age groups;
  • special laboratory subsamples;
  • environmental or nutritional biomarkers.

When a variable comes from a subsample, the corresponding subsample weight may be required.

WarningDo not substitute a broader weight for a subsample weight

A participant may have a valid MEC weight but no probability of selection into a smaller laboratory subsample.

Using the broader weight can misrepresent the population covered by the analysis.

Use the smallest eligible analytical subsample

Suppose an analysis includes:

age
sex
hemoglobin
fasting glucose

The variables come from different levels:

age and sex        → interview or Demographic sample
hemoglobin         → examination or laboratory sample
fasting glucose    → fasting subsample

The fasting subsample is the smallest eligible sample represented in the analysis.

Therefore, the fasting subsample weight may govern the analysis.

Selection rule: Choose the weight corresponding to the most restrictive component represented by the analytical variables.

Positive and zero weights

Before analysis, inspect the selected weight.

Example:

analysis_data |>
  summarise(
    missing_weight =
      sum(
        is.na(
          selected_weight
        )
      ),
    zero_weight =
      sum(
        selected_weight == 0,
        na.rm =
          TRUE
      ),
    positive_weight =
      sum(
        selected_weight > 0,
        na.rm =
          TRUE
      )
  )

Participants with missing or nonpositive weights may not belong to the population represented by that weight.

Do not replace missing survey weights with:

0

or with the sample mean.

Survey weights do not fix every problem

A correct weight does not automatically correct:

  • an invalid merge;
  • duplicated records;
  • incompatible laboratory methods;
  • incorrect variable coding;
  • inappropriate eligibility criteria;
  • missing analyte values;
  • incorrect cycle harmonization;
  • omitted strata and PSUs.

Survey weighting is one part of the analytical design.

Weighted and unweighted summaries answer different questions

  • Unweighted mean
  • Weighted mean

An unweighted mean answers:

What is the average among the analytical records?

It is useful for:

  • quality review;
  • data inspection;
  • pipeline validation;
  • exploratory analysis.

A weighted mean aims to answer:

What is the estimated average in the target population represented by the survey design?

It requires:

  • the appropriate weight;
  • strata;
  • PSUs;
  • valid analytical eligibility;
  • design-based estimation.

Weighting is not the same as multiplying values

A survey-weighted mean is not calculated by multiplying each measurement by its weight and then taking an ordinary mean.

The basic conceptual form is:

sum(value × weight) / sum(weight)

However, valid uncertainty estimation also requires the complex survey design.

In R, this should normally be handled through a survey-design object rather than manual calculations.

Create the survey-design object

A general design structure is:

library(
  survey
)

nhanes_design <- survey::svydesign(
  ids =
    ~ SDMVPSU,
  strata =
    ~ SDMVSTRA,
  weights =
    ~ selected_weight,
  nest =
    TRUE,
  data =
    analysis_data
)

Then estimate a mean:

survey::svymean(
  ~ hemoglobin,
  design =
    nhanes_design,
  na.rm =
    TRUE
)

The placeholder:

selected_weight

must represent the weight verified for the analytical variables and eligible sample.

Combining multiple cycles

When several two-year cycles are combined, the original two-year weights generally require adjustment.

For a standard combination of equally represented two-year cycles, a common conceptual rule is:

multi-cycle weight =
two-year weight / number of two-year cycles

For ten two-year cycles:

analysis_data <- analysis_data |>
  mutate(
    ten_cycle_weight =
      WTMEC2YR / 10
  )
WarningDo not apply this rule automatically

Always review official cycle-specific guidance.

Special considerations may apply when:

  • combining early NHANES cycles;
  • using a preconstructed multi-year weight;
  • combining unequal periods;
  • using nonconsecutive cycles;
  • working with a component-specific subsample;
  • survey designs or target populations changed;
  • the release period was not a standard two-year cycle.

Why divide by the number of cycles?

Each original two-year weight is calibrated to represent a two-year population period.

When several cycles are pooled into one analytical period, dividing by the number of equally represented cycles prevents the combined weights from summing to approximately multiple copies of the target population.

This rescaling generally changes:

  • weighted totals;
  • the scale of the weights.

It generally does not change:

  • weighted means;
  • weighted proportions;
  • regression coefficients that are invariant to a common scaling factor.

The survey documentation and analysis objective still determine the correct procedure.

Do not choose a weight by variable availability alone

A dataset may contain several weight variables.

Their presence does not prove that all of them are appropriate for the analysis.

The selection should be based on:

research question
target population
variables used
component eligibility
subsample restrictions
cycles included
official documentation

Another decision challenge

An analysis includes:

age
sex
hemoglobin

No fasting or special subsample variable is included.

Which weight is the likely starting point?

NoteReveal the answer

The MEC examination weight is the likely starting point because hemoglobin is a laboratory measurement associated with examined participants.

Confirm this against the CBC documentation and eligible population for each survey cycle.

Common survey-weight mistakes

WarningMistake 1: Using the interview weight for laboratory analysis

The interview sample is broader than the examined or laboratory-eligible sample.

WarningMistake 2: Using the MEC weight for a smaller subsample

A fasting or component-specific subsample may require its own weight.

WarningMistake 3: Choosing the weight before listing all variables

A later-added variable may come from a smaller analytical subsample and change the correct weight.

WarningMistake 4: Treating missing weights as ordinary missing data

A missing weight may indicate that a participant was not eligible for the represented sample.

WarningMistake 5: Dividing every weight by the number of cycles without review

Special release periods and component-specific guidance may require a different approach.

WarningMistake 6: Using weights without strata and PSUs

Weights address population representation, while the complete survey design is needed for valid uncertainty estimation.

Quick knowledge check

NoteWhat does a larger survey weight generally mean?

The participant represents more people in the target population.

NoteAre interview and MEC weights interchangeable?

No. They represent different eligible samples.

NoteWhich component usually determines the weight in a mixed analysis?

The most restrictive or smallest eligible analytical subsample represented by the included variables.

NoteShould missing survey weights be replaced with zero?

No. Missingness may indicate that the participant does not belong to the population represented by that weight.

NoteDo weights alone provide valid confidence intervals?

No. Strata, PSUs, and design-based estimation are also generally required.

NoteIs dividing a two-year weight by the number of cycles always correct?

No. It is a common standard-cycle approach, but official cycle-specific and component-specific guidance must be reviewed.

Page summary

The appropriate NHANES survey weight depends on:

the target population
the variables used
the component and subsample
the cycles included
the official analytical guidance

The most important selection principle is:

use the weight corresponding to the most restrictive eligible sample represented in the analysis

A defensible weighted analysis also requires:

strata
primary sampling units
valid analytical eligibility
correct cycle adjustment
design-based estimation
