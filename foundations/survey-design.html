NHANES Survey Design

Understand how the sample is selected and why the design affects analysis

Learn the core features of the NHANES complex survey design, including multistage sampling, strata, primary sampling units, oversampling, clustering, and design-based variance estimation.

NHANES is not a simple random sample

NHANES uses a complex, multistage probability design to select participants from the United States population.

That design affects:

  • who enters the sample;
  • how much each participant represents;
  • how uncertainty is estimated;
  • whether results can be generalized to the target population.

Ignoring the survey design may leave a point estimate looking reasonable while producing incorrect standard errors, confidence intervals, and significance tests.

The four core design features

1

Multistage sampling

Participants are selected through several linked stages rather than directly from one national list.

2

Stratification

The population is divided into design strata before sampling to improve efficiency and representation.

3

Clustering

Participants are sampled within geographic clusters, so observations are not fully independent.

4

Oversampling

Selected population groups may be sampled at higher rates to support reliable subgroup estimates.

A simplified sampling pathway

Stage 1 Select geographic areas
Stage 2 Select smaller geographic segments
Stage 3 Select households
Stage 4 Select eligible individuals

This is a simplified conceptual view.

The exact design details vary by survey period, but the key principle remains:

participants are selected through nested sampling stages

Why multistage sampling is used

A complete national list of all individuals is not usually available or practical for direct sampling.

Multistage sampling allows NHANES to:

  • recruit participants efficiently;
  • organize mobile examination center operations;
  • cover diverse geographic areas;
  • control fieldwork costs;
  • support national inference through probability-based selection.

Core principle: The sample is operationally efficient because people are selected within geographic clusters, but clustering changes how statistical uncertainty must be calculated.

Stratification

Stratification divides the population into groups before sampling.

The goals may include:

  • improving geographic coverage;
  • increasing sampling efficiency;
  • ensuring representation of important population segments;
  • reducing sampling variance.

In public-use NHANES files, the masked variance stratum variable is commonly:

SDMVSTRA

This variable must be included in survey-design specifications used for variance estimation.

Primary sampling units

A primary sampling unit, or PSU, is a major geographic sampling unit selected during the survey design.

In public-use NHANES files, the masked variance PSU variable is commonly:

SDMVPSU

Participants within the same PSU may be more similar to one another than participants selected from different PSUs.

This similarity is called:

intracluster correlation

Why clustering matters

Consider two samples of 1,000 participants.

Sample A

Participants are selected independently across the country.

Sample B

Participants are concentrated within a small number of geographic clusters.

Even though both samples contain 1,000 records, Sample B may contain less independent information.

The effective information content can therefore be smaller than the raw sample size suggests.

ImportantSample size alone does not determine precision

A complex survey may have the same number of observations as a simple random sample but a different variance because of clustering, stratification, unequal selection probabilities, and weighting.

Oversampling

Oversampling means that selected groups are sampled at higher rates than their share of the total population.

This may be done to improve the reliability of estimates for:

  • age groups;
  • racial or ethnic groups;
  • lower-income populations;
  • other analytically important subgroups.

Oversampling does not mean those groups should count more in national estimates.

Survey weights correct for unequal probabilities of selection.

Core principle: Oversampling improves subgroup precision, while weights restore appropriate population representation.

Design variables and weight variables are different

These roles should not be confused.

  • Survey weight
  • Stratum
  • PSU

A weight reflects how much of the target population a participant represents.

Examples may include:

WTINT2YR
WTMEC2YR
component-specific subsample weights

The stratum variable identifies the masked design stratum used for variance estimation.

Common public-use variable:

SDMVSTRA

The PSU variable identifies the masked primary sampling unit used for variance estimation.

Common public-use variable:

SDMVPSU

How the design affects an estimate

Suppose you calculate mean hemoglobin.

The estimated mean depends primarily on:

  • participant values;
  • analytical eligibility;
  • survey weights.

The standard error depends on:

  • weights;
  • strata;
  • PSUs;
  • clustering;
  • the survey estimation method.

Using weights without strata and PSUs may still produce an incomplete design specification.

Simple analysis versus survey analysis

Ordinary analysis

Treats all records as independent and often assumes equal selection probabilities.

May underestimate or misstate uncertainty

Survey-design analysis

Incorporates weights, strata, PSUs, clustering, and design-based variance estimation.

Supports valid population inference

Descriptive and inferential analyses

Not every NHANES analysis must be survey-weighted.

The correct approach depends on the research goal.

  • Unweighted descriptive analysis
  • Survey-weighted analysis

Useful for:

  • inspecting data structure;
  • validating records;
  • checking distributions;
  • identifying outliers;
  • reviewing missingness;
  • testing code;
  • describing the analytical dataset itself.

An unweighted result describes the observed analytical records.

Required when the goal is to estimate characteristics of the target population.

Examples include:

  • national means;
  • national prevalence;
  • subgroup population estimates;
  • population-level regression;
  • design-based confidence intervals.
WarningDo not describe unweighted results as national estimates

An unweighted mean may be useful for data inspection, but it does not automatically estimate the corresponding U.S. population mean.

A basic survey-design specification in R

A future analysis may use the survey package.

A general structure is:

library(
  survey
)

nhanes_design <- survey::svydesign(
  ids =
    ~ SDMVPSU,
  strata =
    ~ SDMVSTRA,
  weights =
    ~ appropriate_weight,
  nest =
    TRUE,
  data =
    analysis_data
)

Each part has a specific role:

ids      → primary sampling units
strata   → sampling strata
weights  → participant survey weights
nest     → recognizes PSU nesting within strata
data     → analytical dataset

The placeholder:

appropriate_weight

must be replaced with the weight that matches the variables and analytical sample.

Why nest = TRUE is often used

Public-use PSU codes may repeat across strata.

Setting:

nest = TRUE

instructs the survey-design object to treat PSUs as nested within strata.

This helps avoid interpreting repeated PSU codes as the same sampling unit across different strata.

A survey mean example

After creating a valid survey design, a weighted mean may be estimated with:

survey::svymean(
  ~ hemoglobin,
  design =
    nhanes_design,
  na.rm =
    TRUE
)

A subgroup estimate may use:

survey::svyby(
  ~ hemoglobin,
  ~ sex,
  design =
    nhanes_design,
  FUN =
    survey::svymean,
  na.rm =
    TRUE
)

These are structural examples.

The analytical dataset, variables, eligibility rules, and weight must be verified first.

Design degrees of freedom

Complex survey inference depends partly on the number of PSUs and strata represented in the analysis.

A common design-based quantity is:

number of PSUs − number of strata

This can affect:

  • variance estimation;
  • confidence intervals;
  • hypothesis tests;
  • model stability.

A large number of participant records does not necessarily imply a large number of design degrees of freedom.

Subpopulation analysis

A common mistake is to physically delete all records outside the subgroup before constructing the survey design.

For survey analysis, it is often preferable to:

  1. construct the survey design using the complete eligible dataset;
  2. define the subgroup using a survey-aware subset operation.

Example:

adult_design <- subset(
  nhanes_design,
  age_years >= 18
)

This preserves the survey design information needed for valid variance estimation.

Missing values and the survey design

Participants may have:

  • a valid survey weight;
  • Demographic data;
  • no laboratory result for the analyte of interest.

The analytical population must therefore distinguish:

sampled participants
examined participants
eligible participants
participants with valid analyte results

The final survey estimate depends on the population represented by the selected weight and the nonmissing analytical records.

Design challenge

Suppose an analysis uses:

  • Demographic variables;
  • CBC hemoglobin;
  • WTMEC2YR;
  • SDMVSTRA;
  • SDMVPSU.

What should be checked before estimating a national mean?

NoteReveal the recommended checks

Confirm that:

  1. the CBC analyte was measured in the MEC examination sample;
  2. the selected examination weight is appropriate;
  3. the analytical population matches the weight;
  4. cycles were combined correctly;
  5. the multi-cycle weight was constructed appropriately;
  6. SDMVSTRA and SDMVPSU are present;
  7. missing hemoglobin values are handled explicitly;
  8. laboratory methods and units are comparable;
  9. the survey design object was specified correctly.

Common survey-design mistakes

WarningMistake 1: Treating NHANES as a simple random sample

This ignores clustering, stratification, unequal probabilities of selection, and oversampling.

WarningMistake 2: Using weights but omitting strata and PSUs

Weights address representation, while strata and PSUs are also needed for design-based variance estimation.

WarningMistake 3: Assuming a large row count guarantees high precision

Clustering and design degrees of freedom affect the information available for inference.

WarningMistake 4: Removing subgroup records before defining the design

This can discard design information needed for valid subpopulation variance estimation.

WarningMistake 5: Using the same design specification for every analysis

Different components and subsamples may require different weights and analytical eligibility rules.

Quick knowledge check

NoteIs NHANES a simple random sample?

No. It uses a complex multistage probability design.

NoteWhat does SDMVSTRA represent?

It is the masked variance stratum variable used in public-use NHANES files.

NoteWhat does SDMVPSU represent?

It is the masked variance primary sampling unit variable used for design-based variance estimation.

NoteWhy are selected groups oversampled?

To improve the reliability and precision of subgroup estimates.

NoteDo weights alone fully specify the survey design?

No. Valid variance estimation generally also requires strata and PSU information.

NoteWhat does an unweighted result describe?

It describes the analytical records included in the calculation, not automatically the U.S. population.

Page summary

NHANES uses a complex survey design involving:

multistage sampling
stratification
clustering
oversampling
unequal selection probabilities

A defensible population analysis must account for:

survey weights
strata
primary sampling units
design-based variance estimation
appropriate analytical eligibility

Understanding the design is what turns a collection of participant records into a valid population-level analysis.
